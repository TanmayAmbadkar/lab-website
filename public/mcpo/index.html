Learning Value from Every Transformer Layer: Depth-Distributed Critics for RLVR

Mean Multi-Critic Policy Optimization (Mean MCPO) attaches independent lightweight critics to every transformer layer and averages their value estimates to form token-level PPO baselines—requiring one response per prompt and no separate transformer-scale critic.

Tanmay Ambadkar1 Pushkar Patel2 Abhinav Verma1

1Department of Electrical Engineering & Computer Science, Pennsylvania State University

2Sarvam, India

21 November 2026Preliminary Empirical Research Note
Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves language-model reasoning by optimizing responses against outcome-based feedback. A central design choice is how to estimate the baseline used in policy-gradient updates. Conventional proximal policy optimization (PPO) learns a value function, often using a separate critic model. Group-relative methods instead estimate advantages from multiple responses to the same prompt, avoiding a learned critic at the cost of additional sampling. Recent approaches have also demonstrated that a language model's internal representations contain information useful for value prediction.

We investigate a different use of those representations: estimating the expected terminal reward of each token prefix from multiple depths of the policy network. We introduce Mean Multi-Critic Policy Optimization (Mean MCPO), which attaches independent lightweight critics to the transformer layers and averages their value estimates to construct token-level advantages. Each critic receives a causal hidden representation, is trained via binary cross-entropy against the observed terminal reward, and has no gradient path into the policy backbone. The resulting algorithm uses one generated response per prompt and does not require a separate transformer-scale critic.

We evaluate Mean MCPO using Qwen3-4B and a multi-domain subset of GURU, with approximately 10 million generated training tokens per method. On 193 held-out problems, Mean MCPO achieves 73.1% exact accuracy, compared with 69.4% for full-critic PPO, 67.9% for PPO with a single lightweight final-layer critic, 65.3% for a local POISE adaptation, 65.3% for GRPO, and 62.7% for RLOO. Mean MCPO also obtains the highest greedy scores among the evaluated methods on MATH-500, GPQA Diamond, Zebra partial credit, and HiTab. Its training consumes 25.08 logged GPU-hours, compared with 53.98 for the full-critic PPO configuration.

These results indicate that depth-distributed value prediction is an effective alternative to a single critic in the tested setting. The experiments are preliminary: the main comparisons use one training seed, and the contribution of representation diversity has not yet been isolated from the benefits of critic ensembling.

01 · Introduction

Credit assignment across depth

Reinforcement learning has become a common approach for improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models. In RLVR, a model generates a response to a prompt and receives a reward determined by the outcome. Mathematical answers can be compared against reference solutions, executable programs can be tested, and structured outputs can be evaluated using domain-specific verification procedures.

The outcome reward provides a training signal without requiring a reference reasoning trajectory. It also creates a credit-assignment problem. A response may contain thousands of generated tokens, while the verifier often provides only one reward after the response is complete. Policy-gradient algorithms must use this terminal feedback to update the probabilities of the actions that produced the response. The choice of advantage estimator determines how this feedback enters optimization.

PPO typically uses a learned critic to estimate future returns. In language-model training, the critic may be a separate transformer, increasing the number of model parameters and the computational resources required for each training step. A lightweight value head attached to the policy is less expensive, although its predictive capacity depends on the representation supplied to it.

Algorithms such as group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) and REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO) avoid training a critic by comparing multiple responses to the same prompt. These methods use group statistics as baselines, but require repeated response generation. At a fixed rollout budget, allocating several responses to each prompt reduces the number of distinct prompt draws that can be included in an update.

An alternative is to estimate value directly from the policy model's hidden representations. These representations are already computed during ordinary language-model inference and training. POISE, for example, uses internal activation statistics to estimate response-level reward expectations through a lightweight regression model.

Can the hidden representations at different transformer depths provide complementary estimates of the expected reward of a partially generated response?

A transformer computes a sequence of intermediate representations before producing the final token distribution. Each layer transforms the information available from the preceding layers. A value estimator attached to a particular layer can therefore access a representation that differs from those available at other depths. The final representation is optimized for predicting the next token. Earlier and intermediate representations may retain other information relevant to evaluating the current response prefix. This possibility motivates depth-distributed value estimation; it does not, by itself, establish that earlier representations are better predictors or that their errors are independent.

We study whether independently trained value predictors across transformer depth can collectively produce a useful baseline for PPO. Our method, Mean Multi-Critic Policy Optimization (Mean MCPO), attaches a small multilayer perceptron to every transformer layer. Each predictor estimates the expected terminal reward conditioned on the current token prefix, and the arithmetic mean of their predictions is used as the baseline for constructing a token-level advantage.

We evaluate the method against conventional PPO critics, alternative aggregation of the same layerwise critics, group-based policy-gradient methods, and a POISE adaptation under a controlled training-token budget. The experiments address three questions:

  1. Does a mean ensemble of depth-distributed critics improve RLVR outcomes compared with conventional value-estimation architectures?
  2. Does averaging critic predictions before constructing a shared advantage perform differently from constructing separate advantages for each critic?
  3. What computational overhead does depth-distributed value estimation introduce relative to alternative training methods?

The current evidence provides favorable answers to the first two questions in the primary Qwen3-4B experiment. The third reveals a tradeoff: Mean MCPO requires slightly more resources than a single lightweight final-layer critic but substantially fewer GPU-hours than the tested full-critic configuration.

02 · Background

Value estimation in language-model RL

2.1 Language generation as sequential decision-making

Consider a prompt x sampled from a training distribution 𝒟. A language model with parameters θ generates a response y = (y1, …, yT) according to an autoregressive policy:

πθ(y | x) = ∏t=1T πθ(yt | x, y<t)

The state before generating token yt is st = (x, y<t), and the action is the next token at = yt. After generating a response, the verifier assigns a terminal reward R(x, y), and the optimization objective is J(θ) = 𝔼x∼𝒟, y∼πθ(·|x)[R(x, y)]. For a sequence-level reward, REINFORCE provides the gradient estimator:

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼[ R(x, y) ∑t=1T ∇θ log πθ(yt | st) ]

The same observed terminal reward contributes to the update at each generated token. For long responses, the estimator can have substantial variance, motivating baseline subtraction.

2.2 State-dependent baselines

A baseline b(st) can be subtracted from the return to construct At = R(x, y) − b(st), yielding the policy-gradient term (R(x, y) − b(st)) ∇θ log πθ(yt | st). When the baseline is independent of the current action conditioned on the state, its contribution to the expected policy gradient is zero:

𝔼at ∼ πθ(·|st)[ b(st) ∇θ log πθ(at | st) ] = 0

A natural baseline is the state-value function Vπ(st) = 𝔼π[R(x, y) | st], which estimates expected terminal reward from the current prefix. This expectation is generally unknown and must be learned. A useful estimator should distinguish prefixes from which successful completion is likely from those with a lower expected probability of success.

The purpose of the baseline is to reduce the variability of the policy-gradient estimator. A value prediction is not itself a measure of whether a particular generated token was causally responsible for the outcome. There is also an important mathematical distinction: subtracting a constant conditional on the state does not reduce the conditional variance of the return itself. Variance reduction concerns the gradient estimator, which includes the policy score function; the state value is a practical baseline, although the exact minimum-variance baseline can depend on the score-function magnitude.

2.3 Conventional PPO

PPO constructs a surrogate objective that limits the change in action probabilities between the rollout policy and the updated policy. For a policy ratio rt(θ) = πθ(at | st) / πθold(at | st), the clipped objective is:

LPPO(θ) = 𝔼t[ min(rt(θ) At, clip(rt(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) At) ]

The estimated advantages are treated as fixed targets during the policy update. A conventional actor-critic implementation estimates At using a learned value network. In RLVR with terminal rewards, the value model can be trained directly against the observed outcome or used within temporal-difference and generalized-advantage estimators. Our approach uses a Monte Carlo terminal-return target for the critics.

2.4 Group-based advantage estimation

GRPO estimates relative performance using several responses generated for the same prompt. A group of rewards provides a reference against which each response is evaluated, and its normalized group-relative advantage can be used in a PPO-like clipped objective. RLOO similarly generates multiple responses, constructing a leave-one-out baseline from the rewards of the other responses in the group.

Both methods eliminate the need for a separately trained value model, but group sampling introduces two consequences. First, repeated sampling uses part of the available token budget to generate additional responses to prompts already in the batch, whereas a one-response method can allocate the same number of sampled responses across more distinct prompt draws. Second, group advantages may become degenerate when all sampled responses receive identical rewards, causing the actor update to be skipped in some implementations. These considerations motivate learning a useful baseline without requiring multiple responses per prompt.

03 · Method

Mean MCPO: depth-distributed value estimation

3.1 Architecture

Consider a causal transformer with L layers (for Qwen3-4B, L = 36). Let hℓ,t = Hℓ(st) denote the representation available from layer ℓ at the state preceding action at. We attach an independent value head to each transformer layer:

Vφℓ(st) = fφℓ(stopgrad(hℓ,t)),   ℓ = 1, …, L

Each value head is a lightweight multilayer perceptron with hidden width 256. The heads have independent trainable parameters and produce one scalar estimate per token prefix. All critics estimate the same quantity: expected terminal reward under the current policy, conditioned on the current prefix.

Because the representation supplied to each critic is detached from the policy computation graph, gradients from the value-prediction objective update only the critic parameters and do not directly alter the transformer backbone. The policy continues to receive gradients from the PPO objective. This separation allows the critics to adapt to the hidden representations produced by the changing LoRA-updated policy without introducing an additional value-loss gradient into the actor.

3.2 Learning from terminal rewards

For a sampled response y, each critic receives the observed terminal reward R(x, y) ∈ [0, 1] as its supervision target. Rather than MSE training or bootstrapped GAE, the critics are trained via Monte Carlo reward regression with binary cross-entropy (BCE) on terminal rewards, together with auxiliary per-head BCE:

ℒℓ(φℓ) = −𝔼(x,y),t[ R(x, y) log Vφℓ(st) + (1 − R(x, y)) log(1 − Vφℓ(st)) ]
Monte Carlo reward regression with binary cross-entropy (plus auxiliary per-head BCE); no intermediate process rewards or bootstrapped GAE targets are used.

All token prefixes from the sampled response are supervised using the realized terminal reward. A single outcome cannot determine the true expected reward of a prefix, but across training samples the proper-scoring BCE objective encourages predictions toward the conditional expected return. The approach requires no intermediate process rewards, annotated reasoning steps, or learned reward model, and the diversity of the predictors comes from their distinct depth inputs and independently learned parameters.

3.3 Aggregating value predictions

The central operation in Mean MCPO is arithmetic averaging across all L critics, V̄(st) = (1/L) ∑ℓ=1L Vφℓ(st), yielding the token-level advantage:

AtMCPO = R(x, y) − 1L ∑ℓ=1L Vφℓ(st)
Every critic contributes equally to the shared baseline, which enters the standard clipped PPO objective with no learned gating network or policy-sized value model.

The difference between successive prefix values can reflect changes in estimated completion success as the response develops; however, Mean MCPO uses these estimates strictly as baselines rather than as an explicit process-reward model.

3.4 Why use multiple layers?

A single final-layer critic operates on one representation from the policy, whereas Mean MCPO accesses representations produced throughout the model's computation. If layers differ in how accurately their hidden states support reward prediction or make imperfectly correlated errors across problems and token positions, averaging may reduce sensitivity to individual critic errors. Let εℓ,t = Vφℓ(st) − Vπ(st) be the prediction error at a particular prefix, and ε̄t = (1/L) ∑ℓ=1L εℓ,t. If each head has error variance σ2 and pairwise error correlation ρ, then:

Var(ε̄t) = 1L2 ∑ℓ=1L ∑k=1L Cov(εℓ,t, εk,t) = σ2 [ ρ + 1 − ρL ]

Independent errors would produce variance reduction proportional to L, whereas strongly correlated errors provide much less benefit. This expression concerns value-prediction error variance rather than the full policy-gradient estimator and does not account for systematic bias; we therefore treat error diversity as a plausible hypothesis rather than an established mechanism.

3.5 Mean MCPO versus Individual MCPO

We also evaluate an alternative aggregation using the same layerwise critics. Whereas Mean MCPO averages predictions first (Atmean = R − (1/L) ∑ℓ Vφℓ(st)), Individual MCPO normalizes each head's advantages over all valid tokens in the effective batch, clips each head's PPO surrogate separately, and averages across heads:

L = −1B ∑i ∑t mit 1K ∑k min(ρit Ãitk, clip(ρit) Ãitk)
Implemented Individual MCPO reduction: mit masks valid tokens, Ãitk denotes head-k advantages normalized over all valid tokens across the effective batch, and the actor loss sums tokens and divides by trajectory count B rather than per-trajectory length.

This is a distinct objective from ordinary PPO with a mean baseline for two reasons. First, because PPO clipping is nonlinear, separately clipping mixed-sign head advantages differs from clipping their mean (verified via a direct numerical check). Second, per-head normalization aggregates over all valid tokens across the batch—so longer responses contribute more observations to normalization statistics—and Mean MCPO does not apply this same advantage normalization. Consequently, the Mean vs. Individual MCPO comparison changes both aggregation order and advantage normalization.

3.6 Causality, implementation audit, and baseline independence

A key distinction between Mean MCPO and response-level internal-state baselines concerns information flow: a token-prefix critic must receive only st = (x, y<t) when forming the baseline for token yt, and current-batch critic updates must not leak into that batch's actor advantages. Auditing the current training source verifies both conditions while highlighting three qualifications on the mathematical description and POISE comparison:

04 · Experimental setup

Models, data, hyperparameters, and controls

4.1 Base model and training data

The primary experiments use Qwen3-4B, a 4-billion-parameter causal language model with 36 transformer layers; all methods start from the same checkpoint. Training uses a multi-domain subset of the GURU reasoning corpus (the full published GURU dataset contains roughly 92,000 examples across math, coding, science, logic, simulation, and tabular reasoning). Our primary study uses a smaller mixture: 1,448 training problems across five reasoning domains and 193 held-out evaluation problems, with science excluded from the held-out GURU evaluation reported here. Each method trains for approximately 10 million generated response tokens. This equalizes a major component of sampling cost, though it does not equalize GPU-hours, actor updates, distinct prompt draws, or responses per prompt.

4.3 Compared methods

  • Starting model. The base Qwen3-4B checkpoint prior to RLVR training.
  • Mean MCPO. Independent lightweight width-256 MLP critics attached to all 36 layers, averaged into one token-prefix baseline.
  • Individual MCPO. Same 36 depth-distributed critics, with critic-specific advantages aggregated across separate clipped PPO objectives.
  • Final-head PPO. A single lightweight critic reading the policy's final-layer representation—the cleanest control for multi-layer value prediction.
  • Full-critic PPO. A separate transformer-scale critic trained for token-value estimation.
  • RLOO. Critic-free REINFORCE with leave-one-out baselines (2 responses per prompt).
  • GRPO. Group-relative policy optimization (4 responses per prompt).
  • POISE adaptation. Local implementation preserving POISE's paired cross-rollout internal-state baselines, but with material differences from the official estimator: (i) the official ridge pipeline includes feature standardization, whereas ours does not; (ii) ours uses a 16-update warm-up vs. the official default of 8; (iii) ours allocates a 4,096-row buffer to every domain vs. the official 4,096 / 3,072 / 3,072; and (iv) ours refits PCA as buffers change with a minimum of 65 domain observations before fitting (initialization equivalence remains untested). Our numbers therefore do not establish superiority over an implementation-faithful POISE baseline.

4.4 Evaluation protocols

We report two external evaluation regimes: greedy evaluation (deterministic decoding, 16,384-token response cap) and sampled evaluation (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, 8,192-token cap, aligned with POISE-style settings). Sampled AIME 2025 uses 32 responses per question; all other external entries use one response per question. GURU evaluation uses greedy decoding with the 16,384-token cap. Because the two protocols differ in both stochasticity and output-length cap, numerical differences between them should not be attributed to temperature alone.

05 · Primary experiment

Results on held-out GURU problems

Across the 193 held-out GURU problems, Mean MCPO attains 141/193 exact successes (73.1%) and 74.5% mean reward (which includes partial credit), leading every tested baseline under the ~10M generated-token budget.

Interpreting the key controls

Vs. full-critic PPO (69.4% exact, 69.9% reward): Mean MCPO improves both metrics while eliminating the separate transformer-scale critic. This demonstrates competitive end-to-end policy optimization, though verifying lower value-prediction error itself requires direct critic calibration measurements.

Vs. final-head PPO (67.9% exact, 69.0% reward): Final-head PPO shares the actor's hidden representations and avoids a separate critic model, controlling for much of the architectural difference. The +5.2 pp margin supports the multi-layer critic construction, but does not yet separate depth diversity from critic count—a matched 36-head final-layer ensemble remains necessary.

Mean vs. Individual MCPO (69.4% exact, 70.6% reward): Both use the same 36-layer critic pathway, yet Individual MCPO trails by seven exact solutions. Because Individual MCPO applies batch-wide per-head token advantage normalization and clips each head's surrogate separately before averaging—whereas Mean MCPO averages predictions without that normalization—the gap reflects both aggregation order and advantage normalization.

Vs. GRPO (65.3%), POISE adaptation (65.3%), and RLOO (62.7%): Mean MCPO exceeds all three under a matched generated-token budget, though group-based methods allocate multiple responses per prompt and therefore differ in prompt coverage and optimization frequency.

06 · Generalization (Greedy)

Transfer to external benchmarks

To test generalization beyond GURU, we evaluate all checkpoints on seven external benchmarks spanning math (MATH-500, AIME '24, AIME '25), science (GPQA Diamond), structured logic (Zebra partial credit), tabular reasoning (HiTab), and instruction following (IFEval). Under greedy decoding (16,384-token cap), Mean MCPO leads on four of the seven external benchmarks: MATH-500 (93.0%), GPQA Diamond (57.58%), Zebra partial credit (27.14%), and HiTab (69.4%).

11 · Relation to prior work

Positioning within RLVR baselines

PPO and actor-critic methods: Mean MCPO retains the standard clipped PPO surrogate objective and should be framed specifically as a critic architecture and advantage-estimation construction within PPO.

GRPO and RLOO: Whereas group methods eliminate learned critics by sampling multiple responses per prompt, Mean MCPO learns prefix-dependent baselines and operates with one response per prompt—changing prompt allocation, update frequency, and compute profile.

POISE and internal-state value estimation: The closest conceptual relative is POISE, which demonstrates that a policy's internal activations and token entropy support lightweight reward regression using independent response pairs and cross-rollout baselines to maintain independence. Mean MCPO instead computes token-prefix baselines using independent MLPs distributed across transformer depth, averaged into a single token-level baseline. Internal-state value estimation itself is not new; the focus here is the depth-distributed construction, its aggregation rule, and controlled empirical comparison.

Value ensembles: Ensembling value predictors is well established in RL; applying it across internal transformer depths leaves open whether gains stem from depth diversity, independent critic initialization, increased capacity, or interaction with PPO clipping.

12 · Mechanisms

What explains the improvement?

The current evidence supports Mean MCPO as a complete algorithm in the primary setting, while leaving four plausible mechanisms unresolved:

  1. Complementary information across depth: Different layers may expose partially complementary signals about prefix completion probability. Testing this requires measuring held-out per-layer and ensemble regression error against repeated-rollout estimates of true expected return.
  2. Variance reduction via ensembling: Independent critics may reduce error variance even when reading identical representations. A matched 36-head final-layer ensemble will directly separate ensembling from depth diversity.
  3. Nonlinear PPO clipping & normalization dynamics: Mean MCPO outperforming Individual MCPO reflects both aggregation order (clipping a mean advantage vs. averaging separately clipped mixed-sign head surrogates) and advantage normalization (Individual MCPO normalizes each head over all valid batch tokens, whereas Mean MCPO does not). Measuring clipping fractions, inter-head gradient cosine similarity, sign conflicts, and isolating normalization will clarify which factor dominates.
  4. Prompt allocation and effective update count: Relative to GRPO/RLOO, single-response rollouts visit more distinct prompts per token budget and avoid degenerate-group skipped updates. Final-head PPO controls for rollout count, while dedicated prompt-allocation experiments would further decouple these effects.
13 · Limitations

Current scope and caveats

  • Single training seed: Primary comparisons use one seed per method. Both paired per-problem confidence intervals and multi-seed replications are needed to establish statistical reliability.
  • Limited architecture diversity: Evidence is restricted to Qwen3-4B (primary) and Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (exploratory). Replication on OLMo, Gemma, and Llama is needed to test architectural generality.
  • Unisolated depth vs. ensemble effect: Final-head PPO controls for single-backbone critics, but not for critic head count. A 36-head final-layer control is still pending.
  • Unmeasured value-prediction accuracy: Downstream policy accuracy alone does not prove lower value MSE or better calibration; direct critic error diagnostics remain to be logged.
  • Rollout allocation & evaluation sensitivity: Equal generated-token budgets do not equalize distinct prompt draws or actor updates; AIME contains only 30 questions per year; Zebra partial credit differs from exact solves; and greedy vs. sampled caps differ.
  • Code audit & checkpoint provenance: Source auditing verifies that rollout advantages are computed and detached prior to critic fitting and that value features and logits align at prompt_width − 1 (with prefix-only vs. full-sequence hidden-state equivalence checked on a reduced SmolLM3 model). However, exact historical reproducibility requires tying each saved checkpoint to an archived source revision, and the POISE adaptation differs materially from the official estimator (no feature standardization, 16-update vs. 8-update warm-up, uniform 4,096 buffers vs. 4,096/3,072/3,072, and dynamic PCA refitting after 65 observations), so our results do not establish superiority over an implementation-faithful POISE baseline.
14 · Planned experiments

Six follow-up studies

  1. Multi-seed reproducibility: Repeat Mean MCPO, final-head PPO, full-critic PPO, and top baselines across independent seeds, reporting learning curves and paired per-problem intervals.
  2. Depth vs. ensemble size: Compare single-layer, subset-layer, and all-layer critics against matched-parameter multi-head final-layer ensembles.
  3. Layerwise value diagnostics: Measure held-out per-layer MSE, ensemble MSE, and pairwise error correlations across domains and token positions to test whether all layers help equally.
  4. Longer training horizons: Extend beyond 10M generated tokens to verify whether advantages persist through convergence as a function of both tokens and GPU-hours.
  5. Cross-model replication: Replicate the training protocol on at least one non-Qwen model family.
  6. Optimization diagnostics: Log critic value distributions, advantage sign agreement, PPO clipping fractions, gradient norms, and completed actor updates—directly illuminating the Mean vs. Individual MCPO gap.
15 · Conclusion

Summary

We investigated whether transformer representations at multiple depths can support token-prefix value estimation for RLVR. Mean MCPO attaches independent lightweight critics to the policy's transformer layers and averages their predictions into a shared token-dependent baseline for PPO.

In the primary Qwen3-4B experiment (~10M generated tokens), Mean MCPO achieves 73.1% exact accuracy on 193 held-out GURU problems—exceeding full-critic PPO (69.4%), Individual MCPO (69.4%), final-head PPO (67.9%), GRPO (65.3%), a local POISE adaptation (65.3%), and RLOO (62.7%)—while also leading greedy scores on MATH-500, GPQA Diamond, Zebra partial credit, and HiTab, generating the shortest average responses on seven of eight benchmarks, and using 53.5% fewer logged GPU-hours than full-critic PPO.

Bottom line: Depth-distributed lightweight critics offer a strong, compute-efficient baseline design for RLVR in the studied setting. Multi-seed replications, matched final-layer ensembles, direct layerwise value diagnostics, and non-Qwen replications remain necessary to isolate the mechanism and establish broader generality.
